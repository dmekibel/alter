*Extracted for: the GOALS menu redesign (statue/creature embodiment — carve strip, 3 numbers, obstacle line, passes list, next-action CTA). David's brief: "SMART-style intelligence in goal creation, the next-step line always a real-life action, the whole engine grounded in KB thinkers." This lane mines Cal Newport's goal/project management machinery for MECHANISMS WITH TEETH — things that change what David does Monday morning, not vibes.*

## The headline finding

Every existing Newport→ALTER doc mines him for the **daily loop and journey stones**. Nobody has yet pointed his goal-management machinery specifically at a GOALS LIST surface — a menu where you create, hold, and advance a bounded set of named ambitions. That's actually his best-fit territory: Newport's entire corpus is a theory of **which goals deserve your attention and how to structure the ones that make the cut.** His "Productivity Funnel" (Selection → Organization → Execution) says the leverage point of any goal system is the top — deciding what gets IN — and that's exactly the gap in a menu that currently just lists cards. A goal menu that only lets you create and unfold cards is doing Organization; it isn't doing Selection. David's complaint ("just because we made cute animals doesn't mean this menu is simplistic") is, in Newport's own language, the difference between a to-do list (infinite room, no reality check) and a calendar (finite room, forces confrontation). The statue system needs a **finite-room mechanic**, not just prettier cards.

The second finding: Newport gives ALTER's "next-action CTA" its actual validity test. His plan-trust law — "the brain is a plan evaluation machine and withholds motivation when it doesn't trust the plan" — means a next-step line is only real if it would survive being said out loud to a skeptical Monday-morning version of the user. Vague verbs ("work on your business," "get healthier") get silently discounted before the user starts. Newport supplies a concrete transformation rule for turning a vague ambition into a next-step the brain will actually start.

## Ranked mechanisms (menu mechanics, most load-bearing first)

### 1. The Selection Gate — a hard cap on concurrently-active statues, with a forced trade-off
**Source:** Newport, Productivity Funnel (CORE-DESIGN Move 5; JOURNEY Stone 4's WIP-cap; SYNTHESIS 1E "Selection layer / WIP cap").
**What it is:** Newport's single sharpest claim: "activity selection gets ignored, even though it's the very top of the funnel — so it impacts everything below." Optimizing Organization (a nicer card) or Execution (more passes) on an overloaded goal list just organizes the overload. The fix is upstream: cap concurrent commitments and make adding a new one a conscious trade-off, not a free action. Newport's own number: 1–2 "major focus" goals max, the rest baselined. The 20%-overload paradox explains why people resist this — they carry ~20% too much specifically as cover to say no to new asks.
**Menu mechanic:** Cap active (currently-carving) statues at 2–3. Creating a new goal while at cap doesn't get silently added — it forces one explicit screen: "Which statue pauses, or which do you finish first?" The old goal doesn't die; it demotes to a **Baseline** state (a lightweight maintenance shelf, still visible on the island, not consuming carve attention). This is the literal "finite room" mechanic the menu is missing. Verbatim gold worth quoting on that screen: *"You baseline almost everything and a very small number of things you put time into."*

### 2. Lead-indicator forcing — every goal must decompose into ONE controllable action before it's allowed to activate
**Source:** SYNTHESIS 1F ("Lead indicators, not lag"); CORE-DESIGN Move 1 (plan-trust).
**What it is:** A stated goal ("get in shape") is a lag indicator — an end-state you don't control day-to-day, which produces anxiety because you're being judged against something unreachable. A lead indicator is the input fully within your control (a count, a duration, a rep) that you can actually do today. Newport: goal capture must force this decomposition or the goal just sits as an aspiration nobody acts on.
**Menu mechanic:** This IS the SMART intelligence David asked for. At goal creation, the menu refuses to accept a goal that's only a lag statement. It asks one mechanical follow-up: "What's the ONE thing you'll do, that's fully in your control, that this goal needs weekly?" That answer becomes the recurring pass-generator — every subsequent pass on the carve strip is an instance of that lead metric, not a freeform list the user has to keep re-inventing. This is also the mechanical guarantee that the next-action CTA is always a real-life action: it's generated from a lead metric, not typed loose.

### 3. The ambition router — ask what KIND of goal this is before generating passes
**Source:** JOURNEY Stone 4 nuance ("is this remarkable, or is it a keystone habit?"); Stone 5 nuance; JOURNEY Stone 6 vs Stone 4 distinction.
**What it is:** Newport draws a hard line between three goal shapes that get mismanaged when treated the same: (a) an **ongoing keystone habit** ("be more disciplined," "meditate daily" — never finishes, judged by consistency), (b) a **pragmatic ambition** (bounded, ~12 months, either achieved or provably not — "I'm going to run a marathon"), and (c) a **transformative goal** (genuinely life-changing, needs backward-chaining — "I'm going to write a novel"). Reverse-planning a habit is absurd; treating a transformative goal like a habit-tracker undersells it.
**Menu mechanic:** Goal creation's first real question isn't the name, it's the shape: ongoing / bounded / transformative. This single branch determines everything downstream — whether the statue ever "wakes" (bounded/transformative) or lives as an evergreen totem (ongoing), whether creation triggers backward-chaining (mechanism #5) or just asks for the lead metric (mechanism #2), and what the "3 numbers" on the unfolded card actually measure (days-of-consistency vs. %-to-bound vs. milestone count).

### 4. The obstacle-line diagnostic — reframe the blocker as structural, not a character flaw, and route to a different fix per diagnosis
**Source:** TOOLS A3 (the fatigue/boredom/confusion branch); CORE-DESIGN Move 4; COPYWRITING pattern "name the false villain, then reveal the real structural culprit" (SYNTHESIS §2).
**What it is:** Newport's signature rhetorical/diagnostic move: when something doesn't get done, don't accept "I'm lazy" — split the actual cause into named categories, because each has a different real fix. His own split for urge-resistance: fatigue (needs a real break, not a fight), boredom/confusion (needs the task redefined, not more willpower), overload (needs the plate reduced, not a pep talk). Applied to goals broadly: the true culprit is almost always overhead, plan-mistrust, or wrong-granularity — never a character deficiency.
**Menu mechanic:** The obstacle line stops being a free-text vibe complaint. When the user names a blocker, the menu offers 3–4 diagnostic buckets (too tired / too big-and-vague / too many other things going / don't trust I'll actually do it) and each routes to a DIFFERENT mechanical fix already in this doc: too-vague → re-run mechanism #2 (shrink to a lead metric); too-many-things → mechanism #1 (the Selection gate, offer to baseline something); don't-trust-the-plan → mechanism #6 (the buffer/calibration nudge); genuinely-tired → a real rest suggestion, not another pass. This is what makes the obstacle line "clever" instead of decorative — it's a router, not a diary entry.

### 5. Reverse-chain with a 30-day lock — for transformative goals only, plan backward but commit forward one month at a time
**Source:** JOURNEY Stone 5 ("Work backwards"); SYNTHESIS 1E.
**What it is:** For genuinely big goals, Newport's method is: name the finished outcome, walk backward through 3–5 real milestones, but **commit in full detail only to the first 30 days** — the rest stays a directional, faded roadmap that gets re-derived once you're actually there. Committing to year-3 specifics now is fantasy, not planning.
**Menu mechanic:** For goals routed as "transformative" (mechanism #3), the carve strip's passes list doesn't populate the whole statue's carve-progression up front. It generates ONE concrete, real-life-action pass batch covering the next 30 days; everything past that renders as a dimmed/faded silhouette on the statue (visually: an outline of future carve-marks not yet real) that only sharpens into real passes once the 30-day block completes. This is a literal UI expression of "don't fantasy-plan year three" and it's a genuinely new visual state for the statue (faded-future vs. carved-real).

### 6. The ground-truth witness gate — before a big goal fully activates, name someone who's actually done it
**Source:** JOURNEY Stone 5 ("ground-truth audit gate"); "hours are not a planning tool... ask them for their story instead."
**What it is:** Before committing years to a transformative goal, Newport requires talking to 2–3 real people who've done it and weighting the plan by what they actually report worked — not the romantic story in the user's head. The naming itself (even before the actual conversation happens) forces reality-contact.
**Menu mechanic:** A single required field at creation for transformative-tier goals: "name one person (real, or a specific book/case you'll study) who's actually done this." This isn't nagging — it's a one-time gate, and it doubles as content: that name can resurface later as the PRIME image before a hard pass ("remember what [name] actually did"). Cheap to build, and it's the single most concrete "SMART intelligence" touch a menu can add without becoming a form.

### 7. Deliberate-practice calibration on every pass — "test at the granularity of your uncertainty," and no credit for vague passes
**Source:** JOURNEY Stone 7 (proof-project, "non-amateur threshold"); TOOLS A1 (reset-to-zero); CORE-DESIGN Move 6.
**What it is:** Two Newport rules combine here. First: a pass (rep) should sit at the edge of current ability — "hard, but not too hard," calibrated to exactly what's uncertain, not a broad vague swing. Second, the harder rule: **"if you're not good at something, it basically doesn't count."** A pass logged as "read an article about it" doesn't earn the same carve-progress as a pass that produced a real, checkable output. Skill-meters that count exposure as progress inflate false competence.
**Menu mechanic:** When a pass is marked complete, the menu doesn't just tick a box — it asks (lightly, one tap) "did this produce something you could show someone?" Passes that did carve deeper into the statue (visually: a sharper/more-defined carve mark); passes that were exposure-only (reading, watching, planning) still count but carve shallower. This protects the "3 numbers" from becoming Goodhart bait — the numbers reflect real deliverables, not activity volume. Newport's own guardrail applies verbatim: *"reward completed depth, not raw activity."*

### 8. The savor gate — the statue waking is not the finish line, it's the start of a mandatory rest
**Source:** JOURNEY Stone 4 ("the savor gate"); CORE-DESIGN Move 6 (pragmatic ambition + mandatory savor phase).
**What it is:** Newport's sharpest anti-optimize-forever mechanic: completing a bounded ("pragmatic") ambition requires a deliberate, ~90-day effort to actually enjoy having it — skipping straight to the next goal in that domain is the exact failure mode that turns healthy ambition into grand-ambition burnout. "When you accomplish a pragmatic ambition you have to put in effort to enjoy the fruits."
**Menu mechanic:** When a statue wakes, the menu doesn't immediately offer "start a new goal in this domain." It locks that domain's "new goal" button for a set savor window and instead surfaces the creature itself, prompts to visit/admire it, and asks periodic "what's actually different now that this is done?" questions. This directly matches the game's own premise (the creature "lives on the island forever") — Newport supplies the mechanical reason the menu should ENFORCE living with it before chasing the next carve, not just cosmetically allow it.

### 9. Circle it, don't launch it — a new, impulsive big-goal idea gets an incubation state before it's a real statue
**Source:** JOURNEY Tier-2 ("Circle it, don't launch it"); MASTER-INDEX shortlist #16.
**What it is:** A guard against "the temporary contact high of change" — a brand-new, exciting ambition gets logged and revisited monthly (read/talk/small-experiment/wait) rather than immediately becoming a fully committed goal. "Getting started is not the bottleneck; it's conviction and knowledge."
**Menu mechanic:** A goal typed in a moment of enthusiasm doesn't have to become a statue immediately. Offer a lighter "circling" state — a small seed/token on the island, not yet a carving statue — that the user is prompted to revisit at a monthly cadence before it's allowed to become an active, carve-tracked goal. This protects the Selection gate (#1) from being gamed by impulse and gives the menu a second tier below "active" that isn't just "baseline" (baseline = a demoted real goal; circling = a not-yet-real one).

### 10. The plan-trust buffer — passes carry a calibration number that improves as the user's own history proves them wrong or right
**Source:** CORE-DESIGN Move 1 (50% buffer heuristic); JOURNEY Tier-2 ("The 50% buffer").
**What it is:** People systematically under-estimate how long things take. Newport's fix isn't a forecasting engine — it's a blunt rule: pad first estimates by ~50%, then let the buffer shrink ~10%/week as real completion data accumulates. The shrinking buffer itself becomes a visible, honest "you're learning to plan" metric — self-knowledge gamified, not output.
**Menu mechanic:** One of the card's "3 numbers" can be a calibration score (estimated vs. actual pass duration, trending toward 1:1) rather than three raw progress counts. This is small to build and gives the numbers row a metric that can't be gamed by rushing — it only improves with honest self-tracking.

## Constitution-level frame (why these, not a bigger menu)

Newport's own Productivity Funnel — Selection → Organization → Execution — should be the one-sentence design brief for this whole menu redesign: **the current goals menu is all Organization (cards) and a little Execution (passes); it has almost no Selection.** Every mechanism above adds a Selection-layer touch (the cap, the router, the circling state, the savor lock) or makes Execution honest (lead-metric passes, calibration, the non-amateur threshold) rather than adding decoration. That's the actual answer to "just because we made cute animals doesn't mean this menu is simplistic" — clever means the menu makes real Selection decisions with the user, not that the cards have more numbers on them.

## Warnings — what Newport would say NOT to do here

- **Don't let the "3 numbers" become a raw activity/aggregate score.** Newport explicitly warns against exactly this (SYNTHESIS/TOOLS both flag a "cognitive-fitness points meta-score" as a Goodhart trap — defer/reject it). Any number on the card must survive: "can this be gamed in a way that hurts the real goal?" Prefer calibration score, lead-metric streak, or carve-depth over a generic XP-style total.
- **Don't build reverse-chaining, the witness gate, or the savor lock for every goal.** These are for bounded/transformative goals only (mechanism #3's router). Forcing a habit-shaped goal ("meditate daily") through a 30-day backward-chain is absurd on Newport's own terms — it "reverse-plans a habit and it feels absurd."
- **Don't let the obstacle line stay a vent.** If it doesn't route to a mechanical fix (mechanism #4), it's therapy-flavored decoration, not the "clever" David is asking for.
- **Don't reward ever-larger totals or remove the cap.** Every Newport ladder in this corpus caps or resets (the 3–4hr deep-work ceiling, the reset-to-zero interval timer). A goals menu that lets you pile on unlimited "active" statues to look productive is the pseudo-productivity trap by name — "the busier you seem, the better" is the enemy metric.
- **Don't fake a fast dopamine hit on a slow goal.** Bounded/transformative goals pay off over months; a badge or confetti animation mid-arc misrepresents the timescale. Newport's fix is a slow-filling arc visual, not a streak.
- **Don't skip the review step before letting a statue "complete."** His shutdown-ritual law generalizes: you can't seal what still has open loose ends. A statue shouldn't fully wake if there are unresolved passes still floating — resolve or explicitly drop them first, mirroring his "capture every loose thread before the close" rule.
- **Don't build restriction. Build the better offer.** If any mechanism here starts to feel like it's blocking/nagging the user out of adding goals, it's drifted off-thesis — the cap (#1) and the circling state (#9) must read as protective ("what matters gets your real attention"), never punitive.
- **Don't over-build the machinery.** Newport's own caution applies directly: "ideally you would not need all these systems." Ship the Selection gate and the lead-metric forcing first (mechanisms #1–2) — they carry most of the leverage. The rest (backward-chain, witness gate, savor lock, circling) are real but secondary; sequence them, don't launch all ten at once.
