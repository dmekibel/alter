# LINEAGE.md — where Headspace's moves actually come from

Built from the 23 caption transcripts in this folder plus the two comparison
scripts (`style3-headspace-andy-reset.txt`, `style2-sam-harris-mindfulness.txt`
in `meditation-scripts/`). All these are YouTube auto or manual captions.
Auto captions have real ASR errors (misheard words); where I quote a line with
an obvious error I've flagged it in brackets rather than silently fixing it.
Timestamps are caption-chunk boundaries, not exact speech boundaries, so
"silence" numbers below are inferred from the gap between one caption's
timestamp and the next, not measured from audio.

A housekeeping note before the sources: three of the files are the same
Kabat-Zinn 30-minute body-scan recording transcribed three separate times
(`kabat-zinn-body-scan-30min.txt`, `kabat-zinn-body-scan-exercise.txt`,
`kabat-zinn-intro-and-body-scan.txt` — the third just keeps an extra ~3 minute
intro the other two cut). They are treated as one source below, not three
data points. Likewise the two Goenka files are the same anapana talk, the
short version and a longer video that repeats it verbatim then adds a live
10-minute sit and a closing talk.

## 1. Per source

### Kabat-Zinn
Files: `kabat-zinn-body-scan-30min.txt`, `kabat-zinn-body-scan-exercise.txt`,
`kabat-zinn-intro-and-body-scan.txt` (same recording, ~28-30 min, auto
captions, clean ASR).

Skeleton (numbered beats):
1. Lie on your back, arms alongside not touching, palms open, feet falling apart
2. Frame the whole practice: this is an invitation to "fall awake," not fall asleep
3. Put out the welcome mat for whatever arises, including unpleasant sensations
4. Let go of wanting things to be different than they are
5. Feel the body as a whole — contact with the surface, air on the skin
6. Attention settles on the breath as a whole (belly or nostrils)
7. Notice when the mind wanders, gently return to breath or body
8. Scan region by region, in fixed order: left toes → sole/heel → top of foot/ankle → lower leg → knee → thigh → right toes → right foot → right lower leg/knee → right thigh → pelvis → lower back → belly/sides → chest/upper torso → hands and arms → neck/throat → head/face
9. At each region: feel sensation or its absence, note pleasant/unpleasant/neutral and any emotional reaction, take one intentional breath "into" it, then on the out-breath let it "dissolve" before moving on
10. Reassemble: hold the whole body in awareness again, rest in stillness until the bell

Signature cues (verbatim, all under 15 words):
- "there's more right with you than wrong with you"
- "it is in the nature of the mind to wander and to think"
- "letting go of the tendency we all have to want things to be different"
- "no one is saying that this is easy"
- "moment by moment and breath by breath" (repeated refrain, dozens of times)

Measured density: ~3,500-3,950 words over ~28-30 min of continuous prose —
roughly 175 words per instructional beat, the densest of any source here.
Longest inferable silence: about 70 seconds near the very end, after "resting
here now as long as you like in silence and stillness until the bells ring."
Talking share: close to 95% — this is almost wall-to-wall narration, the
"silence" is baked into the pacing of the sentences, not into actual gaps.
Lecture vs guided: the `intro-and-body-scan` file opens with ~3 minutes of
program framing (this is a laboratory for practicing being-mode) before the
scan starts; the other two are pure guided scan. One line of the lecture
argument: mindfulness practice trains "being mode" against the pull of the
habitual "doing mode" of mind, and that shift is itself healing.

### Goenka
Files: `goenka-anapana-intro.txt` (15 min), `goenka-vipassana-30min-beginners.txt`
(33 min, same talk repeated then a live 10-min sit added). Auto captions,
moderate ASR quality (some clear mishearings, e.g. "your persona" for what is
almost certainly "your Vipassana").

Skeleton:
1. Frame: everyone seeks peace, everyone gets agitated — lecture
2. The object of awareness is your own natural breath, "the truth of your life"
3. Not a breathing exercise, not pranayama — don't control it, just observe
4. Attention rests at the entrance of the nostrils only
5. When the mind wanders, notice it wandered, return to the breath — no self-judgment
6. No verbalization, no visualization, no imagination — "nothing but truth of breath"
7. Universal accessibility across caste, country, community — lecture
8. Posture: sit comfortably, back and neck straight, eyes closed, glasses off
9. (33-min file only) live guided sit, ~10 minutes, breath-only, very sparse reminders
10. Close: generate loving-kindness — "may all beings be happy... peaceful... liberated"

Signature cues:
- "just remain aware, don't try to change"
- "like a gatekeeper, like a watchman"
- "no imagination is involved, no speculation is involved"
- "work, you have to work your own salvation"
- "may all beings be happy, may all beings be peaceful"

Measured density: the 15-min file is almost entirely dense, continuous talk
(982 words, ~65 wpm, no real silence — this is a lecture, not a sit). The
33-min file's live-sit portion is much sparser: longest inferable silence is
roughly 5 minutes, bounded by the host's own framing ("he's gonna guide for
25 minutes and then we're gonna meditate in silence for another five").
Talking share: ~95%+ for the 15-min lecture; roughly 60% across the full
33-min file once the silent sit is counted in.
Lecture vs guided: mostly lecture. The guided instruction is a single
repeated beat (watch the breath at the nostrils, don't control it) wrapped in
a large amount of dharma-talk framing about agitation, truth, and the ten-day
path. One line of the lecture argument: agitation is the mind's habitual
reaction to desirable/undesirable events, and awareness of the breath — pure
observation with no imagination or belief involved — is the first rung out
of that habit.

### Mingyur
Files: `mingyur-body-space-awareness-guided.txt` (14 min, manual captions),
`mingyur-difficult-moments-guided.txt` (11 min, manual), `mingyur-dont-clear-your-mind-guided.txt`
(17 min, auto), `mingyur-impermanence-guided.txt` (7 min, auto),
`mingyur-mind-like-space.txt` (4 min, auto).

Skeleton (aggregated across the guided files; body-space-awareness and
impermanence carry most of the actual instruction):
1. Spine loosely straight, don't worry about perfection
2. Close eyes
3. Relax muscles region by region: head, forehead, face, ears, cheeks, throat, upper body, arms, lower body, legs, feet
4. Let the mind rest in the body — "let your body be on the cushion, and let your mind be in the body"
5. Notice sensation (pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral), don't try to change it — "just be aware and let it be"
6. Notice the breath changing (in/out, lungs expanding/contracting)
7. Expand awareness beyond the body to sounds, smells, room temperature
8. Expand further to the space around and above you, then to boundless space
9. Slowly open eyes, rest the mind as well as the body (jogging-then-sitting-on-grass analogy)
10. Notice thoughts and emotions as "blah blah blah," let them come, let them go
11. Apply this anywhere, briefly, constantly — "short time, many times, everywhere, anytime"

Signature cues:
- "Let your body be on the cushion, and let your mind be in the body."
- "Just be aware, and let it be."
- "Blah blah blah" — his own label for thought/emotion chatter
- "Short time, many times, everywhere, anytime."
- "Don't fight with the panic."

Measured density: body-space-awareness is 846 words over 14 min, ~60 wpm.
Longest inferable silence: roughly 50-60 seconds (e.g. between "so, with any
type of feeling, just be aware and let it be" and the next cue). Talking
share: roughly 50%.
Lecture vs guided: `difficult-moments`, `dont-clear-your-mind`, and
`mind-like-space` are mostly dharma talk with a short guided segment folded
in (in `dont-clear-your-mind`, only the last ~2 minutes are an actual
breathing practice). One line each of what the lecture content argues:
`difficult-moments` — obstacles become opportunities if you ask "what can I
learn," illustrated with his own panic attacks, his father's death, food
poisoning, and an earthquake that destroyed his monastery. `dont-clear-your-mind`
— meditation isn't emptying the mind (trying not to think about pizza makes
you think about pizza more); it's recognizing an innate well-being that's
"like sky in the Himalayan mountain," unchanged by whatever storms pass
through it. `mind-like-space` — awareness itself can't be polluted by
passing phenomena the way space can't be polluted by clouds, so resting in
awareness rather than the phenomena is what ends the "stock market" of mood.

### Goldstein
Files: `goldstein-15min-guided.txt` (auto), `goldstein-guided.txt` (19 min,
manual), `goldstein-thought-and-emotion-practice.txt` (33 min, auto, mixed
guided + live Q&A).

Skeleton (from the two short guided sits, which are two recordings of
essentially the same teaching):
1. Settle into awareness of sitting posture — "sit and know you're sitting"
2. Breathing in, know you're breathing in; breathing out, know you're breathing out
3. Feel the breath wherever it's vivid (nostrils, chest, abdomen) — let it find its own rhythm
4. When another sensation becomes predominant, let it become the new object of awareness
5. Notice pleasant/unpleasant, and note what happens to a sensation as you attend to it (stronger, weaker, fading)
6. Notice predominant mind states/hindrances as they arise — desire, aversion, sleepiness, restlessness, doubt — and investigate their nature
7. Open the field to sounds — "sound is never a distraction"
8. Open the field to thought and image — note "thinking" or "seeing," check your attitude toward it
9. See the insubstantial, impermanent nature of thought itself
10. Return to breath or body as the anchor whenever unsure what's predominant
11. Periodically check your attitude toward what's arising — grasping, aversion, openness
12. Close: sit with a half smile

Signature cues:
- "Sit, and know you're sitting."
- "Sound is never a distraction. It's just another thing to be aware of."
- "The only power that thoughts have is the power that we give them."
- "When they're seen as not-self, there's no problem."
- "It's often helpful to sit with a half smile."

Measured density: the 15-min file is 845 words (~55 wpm); the 19-min manual
file is only 724 words (~37 wpm) — noticeably sparser. Longest inferable
silence: the 19-min file has a ~2-minute gap (12:17 → 13:42) and several
1-1.5 minute gaps; the 15-min file's longest is about a minute near the end.
Talking share: roughly 35-45% across both — real gaps between cues, not just
pacing.
Lecture vs guided: `thought-and-emotion-practice` is mixed — a dense,
continuous ~14-minute guided sit (similar in density to Kabat-Zinn), followed
by nearly 20 minutes of live retreat Q&A that is pure dharma talk. One line
of the lecture content: direct insight into impermanence often first shows up
*as a thought* ("oh, this is impermanent"), and that's fine — the only
problem is getting lost elaborating on the thought instead of returning to
direct sensation.

### Thich Nhat Hanh
Files: `thich-nhat-hanh-calm-ease.txt` (19 min, manual, only 137 words total),
`thich-nhat-hanh-mindful-breathing.txt` (13 min, auto, mostly lecture),
`thich-nhat-hanh-first-8-exercises.txt` (41 min, manual, pure dharma talk).

Skeleton (the actual practiced structure, from `calm-ease` — five gatha
pairs, each preceded and followed by a bell and a long silence):
1. Breathing in, I know I am breathing in. Breathing out, I know I am breathing out. (label: in, out)
2. Breathing in, my in-breath has become deep. Breathing out, my out-breath has become slow. (label: deep, slow)
3. Breathing in, I calm my body and my mind. Breathing out, I ease everything. (label: calm, ease)
4. Breathing in, I smile. Breathing out, I release and let go. (label: smile, release)
5. Breathing in, I establish myself in the present moment. Breathing out, this is a wonderful moment. (label: present moment, wonderful moment)

`first-8-exercises` gives the broader canonical list this is drawn from (the
Buddha's sixteen exercises on mindful breathing, first eight covered): aware
of in-breath/out-breath; follow the in-breath/out-breath all the way through;
aware of the body; calm the body; generate joy; generate happiness; aware of
a painful feeling; calm the pain — but delivered entirely as dharma talk, not
as a guided sit.

Signature cues:
- "Breathing in, I know I am breathing in. Breathing out, I know I am breathing out."
- "Breathing in, I calm my body and my mind. Breathing out, I ease everything."
- "Hello my little pain. I know you are there. I will take good care of you."
- "The way out is in."
- "Recognizing and embracing. That's the first step of the practice."

Measured density: `calm-ease` is the extreme outlier of this entire set —
137 words spoken across a 19-minute recording, roughly 7 words per minute.
Longest inferable silence: about 3 minutes 15 seconds, between the "calm,
ease" cue at 08:48 and the "smile, release" cue at 12:03. Several other gaps
are 2:40-3:06. Talking share: roughly 5%. This is bell-punctuated silent
sitting with a spoken cue dropped in every few minutes, not a narrated
practice at all.
Lecture vs guided: `calm-ease` is pure guided practice (almost no words at
all). `mindful-breathing` and `first-8-exercises` are pure lecture with the
gathas quoted as illustration, not practiced live. One line of the lecture
argument (`mindful-breathing`): you can only meet the blue sky, a tree, or
your loved ones in the present moment, so mindful breathing is literally
"an appointment with life." One line of `first-8-exercises`: mindfulness
doesn't fight a painful emotion, it recognizes and tenderly holds it the way
a mother holds a crying baby, which lets the emotion "lose some of her
strength" and settle back down.

### Shinzen
Files: `shinzen-see-hear-feel-complete.txt` (57 min, auto, Shinzen himself),
`shinzen-see-hear-feel-jeff-warren.txt` (54 min, auto, a student of
Shinzen's teaching the same technique in a community session, heavy on
framing and live Q&A), `shinzen-subjective-experience-explained.txt` (30
min, auto, badly garbled ASR — this is pure lecture/Q&A on the theory behind
"see hear feel," with no guided practice segment at all).

Skeleton (from `see-hear-feel-complete`):
1. Settle the body, relax spine/jaw/shoulders
2. Notice the natural release on the out-breath — rest in that ease
3. Body scan head to toe, region by region, inviting release at each stop, noting "objective" (non-emotional) sensation
4. Return to the breath at its most tangible point, notice any "flow" or ripple around it
5. Notice body sensation tied to emotion (tight chest = fear, flush = shame) without forcing it — label "feel"
6. Shift to internal talk: pick a word or mantra, locate exactly where you hear it ("hear in")
7. Add a pause between repetitions and notice the silence too
8. Expand outward to "hear out" — the size and shape of the sound space around you
9. Slide attention back and forth between hear-in and hear-out
10. Combine: label whatever arises as "feel" or "hear"
11. Add "see": the blank field behind closed eyes (a resting place), then deliberately evoke an image and locate where you see it
12. Open the eyes to outer sight, notice the evoked image can still be faintly present
13. Free noting across see/hear/feel for several minutes
14. Close with a deep sigh and deliberately mindful stretching, framed as still part of the meditation

Signature cues:
- "note or label" — his own term for the practice's core move
- "location, location, location" — how he describes finding where a sound or image lives
- "that's the path of opening up so wide to the senses"
- "we're integrating all feelings into the somatic modality — they are all feel"
- "keeping continuous contact as best you can"

Measured density: 4,501 words over 57 minutes (~79 wpm), but that average
hides real structure — several deliberately silent "just label for a few
minutes" stretches are embedded inside the narration. Longest inferable
silence: about 87 seconds (51:55 → 53:52). Talking share: roughly 55-60%.
Lecture vs guided: `see-hear-feel-complete` is close to pure guided practice.
`see-hear-feel-jeff-warren` is mixed — roughly the first 11 minutes is
framing/lecture about the technique (dry insight vs. shamatha, why Shinzen
teaches it), then a guided noting sit, then extensive live Q&A. One line of
that lecture content: going straight into noting without first settling into
breath concentration ("dry insight") can be a little "amped" or agitating for
some people, so it helps to start with a few minutes of plain concentration
first if noting alone feels too intense. `subjective-experience-explained`
is pure lecture/Q&A explaining the theoretical map behind the technique (why
mental images, inner talk, and emotional-body sensation each have a
characteristic location) — skip its content beyond that one line; the ASR on
this file is unusually poor and much of it is not reliably quotable.

### Tara Brach
Files: `tara-brach-rain-2024.txt` (11 min, auto), `tara-brach-rain-practice.txt`
(20 min, manual — cleaner and more complete version of the same RAIN
teaching).

Skeleton (from `rain-practice`):
1. Settle, eyes close, a few full breaths (inhale like a balloon filling, exhale releasing)
2. Notice and let go in specific areas — shoulders, hands, belly, heart
3. Let the mind drift with thoughts, then gently land back on the breath
4. Scan your life for a recurring difficult, reactive situation (not something at the level of trauma)
5. Bring it to mind like watching a movie; freeze the frame at the moment you feel most stuck
6. R — Recognize: name or mentally whisper what's most predominant (anger, hurt, fear, shame)
7. A — Allow: pause, make space for it, maybe whisper "yes"
8. I — Investigate: feel where it lives in the body; let posture and facial expression express it; ask "what am I believing right now?"; find the most vulnerable place
9. Ask that vulnerable place what it needs and how it wants you to be with it
10. N — Nurture: hand to heart or belly, offer a message of care (invoking a compassionate figure if your own voice isn't enough)
11. After the RAIN: rest in the resulting sense of presence, notice how it's shifted since the start

Signature cues:
- "Recognize, Allow, Investigate and Nurture"
- "Allow doesn't mean you like it, it means you're acknowledging."
- "What does this place most need right now?"
- "I'm sorry, and I love you." / "I'm not leaving."
- "Don't turn away, keep your gaze on the bandaged place." [quoting Rumi]

Measured density: `rain-practice` is 1,355 words over 20 min (~68 wpm).
Longest inferable silence: about 50 seconds (16:45 → 17:35, and again
18:11 → 18:57). Talking share: roughly 65-70%. `rain-2024` is tighter still
— 851 words over 11 min (~77 wpm), longest silence around 40 seconds,
talking share above 75%.
Lecture vs guided: both files are essentially all guided practice — RAIN
itself is taught inline as the meditation runs, not as separate dharma talk
beforehand. No lecture segment to summarize separately.

### Headspace
Files: `headspace-live-andy.txt` (22 min, live Wednesday session, heavy
framing/preamble before the guided portion), `headspace-reset-decompress.txt`
(10 min, mostly guided), plus the comparison file
`meditation-scripts/style3-headspace-andy-reset.txt` (a clean, pre-organized
transcript of the "Reset Your Mind in 10 Minutes" video with its own style
notes already annotated at the top).

Skeleton (from `style3`, which is the clearest single statement of the
Headspace grammar):
1. Eyes open, soft focus, aware of the space around you
2. A few big deep breaths — nose in, mouth out — notice the body expand/soften
3. Gently close the eyes on an out-breath, pause
4. Ground in the body — weight, contact with the seat or floor
5. Notice sounds without resisting them
6. Notice how the body feels overall (heavy/light, still/agitated) — "not trying to change anything, just noticing"
7. Scan down through the body, letting go of areas of discomfort as you pass
8. Settle on the breath, count 1 (rise) / 2 (fall), attention on the out-breath specifically
9. When the mind wanders, let go of the thought, return to the breath, restart the count at 1
10. Let go of the counting/focus altogether — "let the mind be completely free to do whatever it wants"
11. Return attention to the body, the space around you, and gently open the eyes

Signature cues:
- "Not trying to change anything, just noticing how the body feels."
- "Appreciating that sense of space with each exhale."
- "Let the mind be completely free, to do whatever it wants to do."
- "Focusing on the out-breath. Just noticing that feeling of space."
- (from `headspace-live-andy`) "it's more like plugging back in... connecting and reconnecting with the world."

Measured density: `headspace-reset-decompress.txt` is 777 words over 10 min
(~77 wpm). Longest inferable silence: about 72 seconds (06:32 → 07:44).
Talking share: roughly 50%.
Lecture vs guided: `headspace-live-andy` spends its first ~8 minutes on
framing (COVID-era community message, why he's varying the technique week to
week, permission-giving around anxiety) before the guided portion starts —
mark that stretch as lecture/preamble, not instruction. One line of that
framing: meditation isn't about levels of learning, it's about being
reminded of fundamentals ("it's not about stopping thoughts... we simply have
to acknowledge it, let it go, and come back").

## 2. Headspace lineage table

| Headspace move | Found in these roots? | Root's own wording |
|---|---|---|
| Settle with eyes open / soft gaze | **Not found.** None of the 23 root transcripts open a sit with eyes open. Kabat-Zinn mentions eyes-open only as an anti-drowsiness fallback mid-practice ("it may be helpful to practice with your eyes open from time to time if any feelings of drowsiness arise"), which is a different device. | — |
| Notice sounds | Goldstein, Mingyur, Shinzen | Goldstein: "let the mind open to the awareness of the different sounds as they arise... simply be in the direct experience of hearing." Mingyur: "maybe there are some sounds, or some smells, or some sensations." Shinzen: builds an entire "hear out" stage around exactly this. |
| Body scan head to toe | Kabat-Zinn (this is essentially its origin here), Shinzen, Mingyur (briefly) | Kabat-Zinn: the entire 28-minute practice is a region-by-region scan from left toes to the crown of the head. Shinzen: "let's just allow the awareness to run down the top of the foot... from the top of the head to the tip of the toe." Mingyur: "feel your body... from head to feet." |
| "No need to change anything" | Goenka, Mingyur, Kabat-Zinn (implicit) | Goenka: "don't try to change, just remain aware" — near-verbatim precursor. Mingyur: "you don't need to control sensation, you don't need to change the sensation, you just be." Kabat-Zinn: "allow things to be exactly as you find them." |
| Breath count 1-10 in/out | **Not found.** No root source in this set counts breaths at all — not Kabat-Zinn, Goenka, Goldstein, Mingyur, Shinzen, Brach, or Thich Nhat Hanh. | — |
| Noting thinking and returning | Nearly universal — Kabat-Zinn, Goenka, Goldstein, Mingyur, Shinzen, Sam Harris (comparison file) | Kabat-Zinn: "noticing if there are times when the mind wanders... gently bringing your attention back." Goldstein: "note thinking... it's just another arising object." Shinzen: "note or label." Goenka: "mind wanders away, come back to the natural breath." This is the single most cross-source move in the whole set. |
| "Let the mind be free" before the end | **Not clearly found.** Closest cousin, not a real match: Mingyur's "rest your mind also, not only the body" (letting the mind rest without a technique, not the same device as "let it think whatever it wants"). | Mingyur: "just let your mind rest as it is, and the body, also." |
| Gently open eyes at the end | Mingyur, Shinzen | Mingyur: "please slowly open your eyes, and be present here." Shinzen: "you're going to want to open your eyes very slowly and keep the gaze down as you open the eyes." |

Two of the eight canonical Headspace moves — the eyes-open opening and the
breath count — do not appear anywhere in these 23 root transcripts. That
doesn't mean they have no lineage anywhere (breath counting is a well-known
Zen device, for instance), just that it isn't sourced in this particular
corpus, and the honest answer is to say so rather than force a match.

## 3. The common skeleton

Looking at the five sources that run something like an open, expanding
practice (Kabat-Zinn, Goldstein, Mingyur, Shinzen, and Headspace itself),
the majority order is:

**settle → body → breath → thoughts → release → return**

- **Kabat-Zinn** follows this closely but makes "body" the entire content of
  the practice (a 28-minute scan) rather than a brief stage — thoughts are
  handled as asides ("noticing what is on your mind") rather than a distinct
  stage of their own.
- **Goldstein** follows the order cleanly: posture → breath → other
  sensations → hindrances/thoughts → return to the breath as anchor →
  periodic attitude-check (a "release" of sorts, checking for
  grasping/aversion).
- **Mingyur** inserts an extra stage the others don't have: after body and
  breath, he explicitly expands awareness *outward* — sounds, then the space
  around you, then boundless space — before circling back to thoughts and
  emotions.
- **Shinzen** deviates the most structurally within this group: he treats
  seeing, hearing, and feeling as three fully separate, individually-taught
  channels, and ends not on a "release" but on mindful stretching folded
  into the meditation itself.
- **Goenka** and **Thich Nhat Hanh** don't follow this shape at all — they
  stay anchored on the breath the entire time and never broaden into an open
  awareness of sound, thought, or the body as separate stages. Goenka is a
  single-object concentration practice; Thich Nhat Hanh's gathas are a tight
  four-beat loop (breath → body → emotion/smile → present moment) repeated,
  not an expanding progression.
- **Tara Brach** deviates the most of all: RAIN reorganizes the entire shape
  around processing one specific emotional difficulty. Body only appears
  inside the "Investigate" step, and there's no scan, no sound stage, and no
  general "let thoughts come and go" instruction — the whole practice is
  built to work with one thing, not everything that arises.

## 4. Economy benchmarks

Rough estimates, all inferred from caption timestamps (not audio-measured).

| Source | Words per cue (approx.) | Longest inferable silence | Talking share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kabat-Zinn | ~175 words/beat | ~70 sec | ~95% |
| Goenka (lecture portions) | continuous, ~65 wpm | ~40 sec (within lecture) | ~95% |
| Goenka (live sit, 33-min file) | sparse, short reminders | ~5 min (host-stated silent block) | ~60% (full file) |
| Goldstein | ~15-40 words/cue | ~2 min (19-min file) | ~35-45% |
| Mingyur | ~20-30 words/cue | ~50-60 sec | ~50% |
| Shinzen | ~79 wpm average, with embedded silent stretches | ~87 sec | ~55-60% |
| Tara Brach | ~70-80 wpm, cue = one RAIN-step paragraph | ~40-50 sec | ~65-75% |
| Thich Nhat Hanh (`calm-ease`) | ~10-20 words/cue | **~3 min 15 sec** | **~5%** |
| Headspace | ~77 wpm | ~72 sec | ~50% |

**Thich Nhat Hanh is by far the most economical.** `calm-ease` says 137
words across a 19-minute recording — every gatha pair is under 20 words and
is followed by roughly three minutes of bell-punctuated silence. Nothing
else in this set comes close; even Goenka's stated 5-minute silent block is
a single block, not a repeating pattern, and Kabat-Zinn is the opposite
extreme — almost no silence at all, carried entirely by continuous prose.

## 5. Five things Headspace does not do (candidates for ALTER's advanced levels)

1. **A full sequential body scan that names every region and lets it
   "dissolve" on the exhale**, rather than one quick top-to-bottom sweep.
   Source: Kabat-Zinn — "shift the focus of our attention to the toes of the
   left foot... on the out-breath... watching them dissolve in our mind's
   eye."

2. **A structured protocol for working with one specific, named difficult
   emotion** (not just noticing and returning to the breath). Source: Tara
   Brach — "we begin RAIN by just recognizing... the A of RAIN is allow...
   [then] investigate... nurture."

3. **Treating thought, sound, and sight as separate channels the
   practitioner explicitly names out loud as they occur**, rather than one
   generic "noticing." Source: Shinzen — "you'll note or label... feel... if
   your awareness is drawn to anything visual... it will be sight."

4. **Addressing a painful feeling directly, by name, like comforting a
   child**, instead of just returning attention to the breath. Source:
   Thich Nhat Hanh — "Hello my little pain. I know you are there. I will
   take good care of you."

5. **Explicitly expanding awareness outward past the body into boundless
   space** as its own deliberate stage, rather than staying contained within
   body/breath/thought. Source: Mingyur — "you can expand your awareness
   even more, go beyond the clouds, into space, which is boundless."
