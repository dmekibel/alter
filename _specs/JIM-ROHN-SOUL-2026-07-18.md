All quotes below are verbatim as printed, tagged with source book.

The one idea that runs under everything Rohn wrote, in different clothes every time:

- **Philosophy comes first.** What you believe about how life works decides what you feel, which decides what you do, which decides what you get, which decides how you live. He calls this chain Philosophy -> Attitude -> Activity -> Results -> Lifestyle, and his whole argument is: stop trying to fix Results directly. Go back and fix the cause.
- **You get paid for what you become, not for what you do.** Effort at a fixed skill level just extracts more from the same ceiling. Growing yourself raises the ceiling.
- **Nothing is neutral. It's either growing or going to weeds.** Relationships, discipline, self-education, a garden, a marriage, your own mind. Nobody described this as "maintenance mode." Everything left alone decays.

Everything below is Rohn's way of acting on those three sentences.

## 1. ROHN'S CORE PHILOSOPHY, AS ATOMS

### 1. The Five Major Pieces: Philosophy -> Attitude -> Activity -> Results -> Lifestyle
The idea in plain words: what you believe about how life works (philosophy) shapes how you feel about it (attitude), which shapes what you actually do (activity), which produces results, which determines the life you get to live (lifestyle). It's a straight chain, five links.
The mechanism: "It is our personal philosophy that establishes our individual attitude. It is our attitude that determines both the quantity and the quality of our level of activity. That activity produces proportionate results, and the results provide the lifestyle we seek." Most people try to change the last link (chase a better lifestyle, chase better results) without ever touching the first one. That's why the fix doesn't hold — "most people curse the effect but continue to nourish the cause." Change the belief first; everything downstream moves on its own.
Source: *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle*.

### 2. Discipline's pain is ounces. Regret's pain is tons.
The idea: every day, you pay one of two costs — the small, chosen discomfort of doing the disciplined thing now, or the much larger, unchosen pain of regret later.
The mechanism: both are real pain, so this isn't effort-vs-comfort, it's cheap-pain-now-vs-expensive-pain-later. "We will all experience one pain or the other... but the difference is that the pain of discipline weighs only ounces while the pain of regret weighs tons." Comparing the two costs honestly is what makes the disciplined choice rational, not just virtuous.
Source: *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle* (this is the full, canonical wording); repeated throughout *7 Strategies*, *Twelve Pillars*, *Seasons of Life*, *The Treasury of Quotes*.

### 3. Failure is not an event. It's a few repeated errors that don't seem to matter.
The idea: nobody fails in one dramatic moment. Failure is a habit of small, forgivable-seeming choices, repeated daily, that compound.
The mechanism: "failure is nothing more than a few errors in judgment repeated every day." The danger is precisely that each single instance looks harmless — "the things that are easy to do are also easy not to do." Success runs on the identical mechanism in the other direction: "a few simple disciplines practiced every day." Same structure, opposite direction — which means the fix is never a single big act, it's redirecting the next small daily choice.
Source: *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle*.

### 4. The seasons are a literal cycle, not a metaphor you can skip.
The idea: life runs in four seasons — Spring (plant, take the opportunity, act on faith with no guarantee), Summer (defend and cultivate what you planted, because pests and weeds attack anything of value automatically), Fall (harvest — the truth-telling season, you either have crops or you have excuses), Winter (the storms, external and internal).
The mechanism: Rohn insists this is a law as fixed as sowing and reaping. You don't get to reorder the seasons or skip one — you don't harvest in spring, you don't plant in fall. The season you're in dictates the correct action.
Source: *Seasons of Life*.

### 5. Winter always comes. What decides its character is what you did in the seasons before it.
The idea: "The first great lesson of life to learn is that winter will always come." Hard times are guaranteed for everyone — the planter, the family, the business. The only variable is whether you're prepared.
The mechanism: winter is neutral by itself. If you planted, defended, and harvested well in the prior seasons, winter becomes rest, reflection, gathering strength, teaching others what you learned. If you didn't, winter is regret, an empty storehouse, "horror and uncertainty." Winter doesn't create the outcome — it reveals what the earlier seasons already decided. And winter isn't confined to a calendar slot: it "can make its brief appearance during any season," a storm mid-summer, a setback mid-spring.
Source: *Seasons of Life*.

### 6. Attitude is the one lever you always keep, and it decides the outcome.
The idea: weather, competitors, other people, the economy — all uncontrollable. Attitude is the single thing a person always retains control over.
The mechanism: "Attitude determines choice, and choice determines results... In the cycles and seasons of life, attitude is everything!" Because attitude is the only constant control point, all real self-improvement work reduces to attitude work — not circumstance work. And no one else actually controls your attitude either: "No one else 'makes us angry.' We make ourselves angry when we surrender control of our attitude... They merely put our attitude to a test."
Source: *Seasons of Life*; *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle*.

### 7. Effort now is disconnected from reward now — that's the whole point.
The idea: planting (spring effort) and harvest (fall results) are separated by an entire growing season during which nothing visible happens except unglamorous defense.
The mechanism: this breaks the instant-gratification model. "The seed as well as our needs must await the changing seasons... we cannot ask nature for an advance." Present want cannot shortcut present effort — the only path to a future harvest is present effort applied now, toward a result you won't see for a while.
Source: *Seasons of Life*.

### 8. Nature multiplies. A cup produces a bushel.
The idea: effort invested doesn't return 1:1, it compounds — for every planted cup, many bushels reaped.
The mechanism: this is Rohn's answer to "is it worth it," and it's what justifies effort in a season where you see zero immediate result. The return isn't proportional in the small sense, it's proportional in the compounding sense.
Source: *Seasons of Life*; the parallel line from *The Five Major Pieces*: "Results are always in direct proportion to effort."

### 9. Rock bottom is where most success stories actually start.
The idea: "Nearly every success story that I am aware of began when the person first lay flat on his mental and financial back."
The mechanism: sufficient disgust plus desire plus determination is what actually produces change. Comfort rarely generates that resolve. Hitting bottom functions as an ignition event, not just a low point — "thank God for your present limitations." Rohn's own version of this: broke at 25, lying to a Girl Scout that he already had cookies because he couldn't spare two dollars — the shame of that small lie, not a grand crisis, was his turning point.
Source: *Seasons of Life*; *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness* (the Girl Scout story).

### 10. Work harder on yourself than you do on your job.
The idea: "If you work hard on your job, you make a living. If you work hard on yourself, you can make a fortune."
The mechanism: your income is capped by your current skill. Working longer hours at a fixed skill level just extracts more from the same ceiling — it doesn't raise it. Only investing in yourself (reading, studying the best, applying what you learn) raises the ceiling itself. "Income seldom exceeds personal development... it's hard to keep that which has not been obtained through personal development" — meaning even a windfall won't stick if the person hasn't grown to match it.
Source: *My Philosophy for Successful Living*; *Twelve Pillars*; *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness*.

### 11. Profits are better than wages.
The idea: wages pay you for your time and labor. Profits — ownership, investment, building something — pay you for value created, uncapped by hours worked.
The mechanism: "Wages will make you a living, but profits will make you a fortune." A wage-earner's income is bounded by someone else's ceiling on what an hour of labor is worth. Rohn's practical bridge from wages to profits: live on 70% of after-tax income, split the remaining 30% into three equal parts — charity, capital investment (buying/fixing/selling, becoming an owner), and savings.
Source: *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness*; *Twelve Pillars*; *My Philosophy for Successful Living*.

### 12. The real reason to set a goal is who you have to become to hit it.
The idea: the goal itself is secondary. "The major reason for setting a goal is for what it makes of you to accomplish it. What it makes of you will always be the far greater value than what you get."
The mechanism: skills, discipline, and identity developed while chasing a goal outlast the goal object. That's also why writing a goal down matters mechanically, not just symbolically — the act of writing forces you to actually decide on one, and it makes the mind "cognitively aware," after which it starts working on it automatically. A goal not written down is, in Rohn's words, "at best... a wish, and wishes are little more than self-delusion."
Source: *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness*; *Twelve Pillars*; *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle*.

### 13. Neglect is the real cause of most failure — not lack of resources.
The idea: it's rarely lack of money, opportunity, books, or teachers that stops people. Banks are full of money. Libraries are full of free books. The word that actually explains underachievement is "neglect."
The mechanism: "The things that are easy to do are also easy not to do." There's no forcing function against neglect except self-discipline, which is exactly why it's so common and so dangerous — nothing punishes you the day you skip it. Rohn's Niagara River image for this: a man fishes calmly, lets his mind drift, doesn't notice his boat imperceptibly drifting toward the falls until it's too late.
Source: *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle*.

### 14. Stand guard at the door of your mind.
The idea: what touches you (people, books, conversations, what you let in) actively authors your attitude — it isn't generated in a vacuum.
The mechanism: "We must all wage an intense, lifelong battle against the constant downward pull. If we relax, the bugs and the weeds of negativity will move into the garden and take away everything of value." Since attitude is fed continuously by inputs, curating those inputs is upstream work, before attitude work even starts. "You cannot take the mild approach to the weeds in your garden. You've got to hate weeds enough to kill them."
Source: *The Treasury of Quotes*; *Seasons of Life*.

### 15. Associations: about 20% of the people you spend time with produce 80% of your good results — but they get only 20% of your time.
The idea: who surrounds you shapes what you think, say, and become, subtly and cumulatively, not through one dramatic event. Rohn's own 80/20 statement, the direct ancestor of "you're the average of the five people you spend the most time with": "about eighty percent of the people with whom we associate... will account for only about twenty percent of the results... about twenty percent... will produce eighty percent of the good results" — and the low-value 80% typically consumes 80% of your time.
The mechanism: "Influence is so subtle; it's like a little nudge and each time you get together there is one more nudge in the wrong direction." The fix isn't dramatic either — it's sorting relationships into three buckets: disassociation, limited association, expanded association, based on the honest answer to "who am I around, what effect are they having on me, and is that okay?"
Source: *Twelve Pillars*; *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle*; *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness*.

### 16. Formal education gets you a job. Self-education gets you a fortune.
The idea: school teaches facts and ends. Growth doesn't end there — it has to become a self-directed habit for the rest of life.
The mechanism: most of a life is lived after formal schooling stops, so whatever growth happens in the following decades has to be self-generated. Three mainstays Rohn names: read books, learn by watching successful people, reflect on your own experience.
Source: *My Philosophy for Successful Living*; *Twelve Pillars*.

### 17. The journal is where good ideas live, because your memory is not a filing cabinet.
The idea: keep a personal journal as a gathering place for every idea and observation that comes your way — and specifically capture the feeling attached to an event, not just the fact, because feeling fades fastest.
The mechanism: "Don't use your mind for a filing cabinet. Use your mind to work out problems and find answers; file away good ideas in your journal." There's a second, less obvious mechanism too: the physical act of writing slows down thinking and forces objectivity — you actually see your own philosophy under scrutiny as you write it, which is what makes revising it possible. "The reason why I spend so much money for my journals is to press me to find something valuable to put in them."
Source: *The Treasury of Quotes*; *The Five Major Pieces to the Life Puzzle*; *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness*.

### 18. Plan on paper, at every timescale, before you start.
The idea: four distinct planning tools serve different jobs — a journal (ideas), a project book (a tabbed binder per person or project), a calendar (appointments), and a visual "game plan" (activities against deadlines, kept where you'll see it — office wall, fridge).
The mechanism: "Don't start your day until you have it finished. Don't start the week until you have it finished. Don't start the month until it's finished." Planning is nested — day inside week inside month inside year — and each level has to be closed on paper before the next level starts, or the plan lives only in your head, where it decays.
Source: *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness*.

### 19. The day that turns your life around is often small and a little humiliating, not dramatic.
The idea: the trigger for real change is rarely a grand crisis. It's a specific, personal, sometimes embarrassing moment where "enough is enough" finally has enough fuel behind it.
The mechanism: "mild disgust... there isn't enough fuel in that kind of emotion to propel a toy boat in a bathtub." It has to reach a real point of no return. Rohn's own example is the Girl Scout cookie lie; another is a mentor having him write every excuse on one page, tear it up, and write one word on a fresh page — "me."
Source: *7 Strategies for Wealth and Happiness*.

### 20. Legacy is the pillar that gives the rest of the pillars credence.
The idea: a life aimed only at accumulation, with nothing set aside for the people who come after, is unfinished — legacy isn't a bonus round, it's what proves the philosophy was real.
The mechanism: "We are but breezes of wind that blow through this world... You cannot choose how long you will live, but you can choose how well you will live." The choice is explicitly framed as "making a living" (drifting, no vision) versus "designing a life" (intentional, aimed at leaving something behind). "Leaving a legacy is like planting a tree. As that seed grows into a tree, it will provide seeds, so that future generations can then plant their own."
Source: *Twelve Pillars*.

## 3. ALTER-APPLICATION

Each atom mapped to a concrete, buildable surface in the app. Copy voice for all of these still has to clear `_specs/SCRIPT-ENGINE.md` (concrete or it dies, no em-dashes, no emoji) before anything ships — these are content/mechanic directions, not final lines.

**Philosophy -> Attitude -> Activity -> Results -> Lifestyle (atom 1) -> the journey stone grammar itself.** The existing PRIME -> DO -> NAME -> CHECK -> PLACE -> SEAL lesson grammar is already Rohn's chain compressed into one rep: PRIME plants a belief, DO is the activity, NAME and CHECK make the attitude explicit and honest, PLACE schedules it into the real day (results), SEAL locks it into how the day actually goes (lifestyle). A Rohn-flavored stone should teach this chain directly as its PRIME line: don't fix the result, fix the belief underneath it.

**Discipline vs. regret (atom 2) -> the PM close.** The PM close already asks for one honest observation and carries blocks forward. Add a single optional line at close, when a planned block was skipped: reads the miss not as failure but as a real-time comparison — "ounces now, tons later" reframed in ALTER's own voice, never guilt, always forward. This is a copy addition to an existing surface, not a new mechanic.

**Failure as repeated small errors, not one event (atom 3) -> drift-recovery (offRamp/comebackLadder).** These functions already exist and already follow the reward-never-shame law. The Rohn framing sharpens their internal logic: a single missed rep is never the problem (the app already says this via forward-only framing) — the comeback ladder's one-rung-up design is the literal antidote to "a few errors repeated every day," since it interrupts the repetition at the next occasion instead of moralizing about the last one.

**The seasons (atoms 4, 5, 7) -> a season state on the planner, and winter-aware copy in the tracker.** The planner could carry a slow-moving season indicator (spring/summer/fall/winter) driven by the user's own recent pattern, not a calendar — a stretch of misses and low energy reads as "winter conditions" regardless of the actual month, and the app's tone shifts to Rohn's winter register (rest without abandoning the plan, no self-flagellation, "this one doesn't have to be a big day"). Build law: winter must be detectable at any point in the arc, not scheduled — atom 5's insight that winter "can make its brief appearance during any season" means the drift-detection logic (already present) is the right home for this, not a new calendar system.

**Attitude as the one lever (atom 6) -> the mood/energy gauge framing.** The existing gauge already asks how the user feels before routing them. Rohn's line sharpens the copy under it: the gauge isn't measuring mood as a fact about the day, it's naming the one thing that's actually still in the user's hands when everything else about the day isn't. One line at the gauge, not a redesign.

**Rock bottom as ignition (atom 9) -> the honest-no in the CHECK step.** The stone grammar's CHECK step is already "skeptic-safe, honest-no respected." Rohn's insight adds a design rule: don't chase the user who says no with more persuasion. The turning point he describes is always small, personal, and self-generated — never delivered by someone else's pitch. The app's job at CHECK is to leave room, not close the sale.

**Work harder on yourself than your job (atom 10) -> the tools-first home screen already ships this.** No new surface needed; this is the existing hook (tools -> tracked -> coins -> plan) restated as philosophy. Worth one PRIME line early in the journey naming it directly, since it's the clearest Rohn atom that the app's structure already embodies without saying so.

**Goals are for who you become, write them down (atom 12) -> the goals-setting flow + a "why" capture at goal creation.** When a user sets a goal, capture one line for what completing it would make of them, not just the goal object — surfaced back to them at milestones, not as a nag but as a mirror ("you said this would make you someone who finishes things — look"). Concrete, buildable: one text field, one storage key, one re-surface point at goal-complete.

**Neglect over lack of resources (atom 13) -> the drift-detection copy, reframed away from "you didn't have time."** When the app flags a gap, the honest Rohn line is that the block wasn't missing, the day wasn't missing, attention was. This changes copy tone (never blame external circumstance on the user's behalf, which secretly patronizes) without changing any mechanic.

**Stand guard at the door of your mind (atom 14) -> the sanctuary world's charging/plant mechanic.** Already-shipped: charged marks = glowing plants, hold-to-charge = investment. Rohn's plant/weed language is a near-literal match for the sanctuary's own visual grammar (a garden that grows or goes to weed depending on tending). One copy pass connecting the charging ritual explicitly to "what you let stay charged" would tie an existing mechanic to an explicit teaching without adding scope.

**Associations, 20% for 80% of the results (atom 15) -> not a build now, a content note.** ALTER is single-user (just David), so there's no in-app social graph to sort. The atom still applies to the chronicle/journal: a reflection prompt that occasionally asks who the user spent real time with that week and what it did to their attitude, logged, never scored. Low priority, journal-only.

**Journal as feeling-capture, not fact-filing (atom 17) -> the chronicle.** Rohn's mechanism (feeling fades faster than fact, so capture it while it's fresh) is a direct design spec for the chronicle's prompt copy: ask for the feeling attached to the day's one thing, not a log of what happened. ## 4. LINEAGE

Rohn's material carries an explicit legacy argument inside itself — *Twelve Pillars* ends with its own mentor character revealed, after his death, to have been the source of everything, and the book's final pillar is literally titled "Leave a Legacy."
