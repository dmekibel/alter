# CAL NEWPORT'S SYSTEMS -> ALTER, IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Before anything else, the one idea that runs under all of this. Newport keeps saying the same three things in different clothes:

- **You're not lazy. Your attention got stolen** by machines built to steal it.
- **Your brain won't start a plan it doesn't trust.** Give it a huge messy pile and it quietly refuses to begin. Give it one small honest thing and it goes.
- **Do less, better.** Fewer things, done well, beats many things done in a panic.

Everything below is a way to act on those three sentences.

---

## 1. His actual systems, in plain steps

These are the real Newport systems, stripped down so you could actually do them. I put them roughly easiest-to-hardest.

### A. The Minimum System (his "smallest thing that works")
**What it's for:** the least amount of setup that stops you from missing things and feeling out of control. The floor, not the ceiling.

**Steps:**
1. Get one calendar. Put anything with a specific time on it (appointments, deadlines).
2. Keep one list for everything else (a notes file, a legal pad, a notebook). Write down every to-do here so it's not in your head.
3. Each morning, spend 5 minutes: look at the calendar and the list, pick a few things to actually do today, before you open email.

**His rules:** Exactly three parts, no more: a place for tasks, a sense of how much you can handle, and a daily look before the world grabs you. Nothing lives only in your head.

**Energy:** Low. This is the one almost anyone can start.

### B. Time-Block Planning
**What it's for:** stop drifting between email and random tasks all day; give every work hour a job so you actually finish things.

**Steps:**
1. At the start of your work day, copy your meetings onto one page.
2. Split the leftover hours into blocks. Write one task next to each block ("9 to 10:30: write report").
3. Work the block on that one thing until it ends.
4. When you fall behind (you will), cross out the rest and redraw the remaining hours. Redrawing is normal, not failure.
5. At day's end, stop and switch off.

**His rules:** Every work hour gets a job. When a block blows up, redraw instead of forcing it. There is no gold star for guessing the time perfectly. Don't time-block weekends.

**Energy:** Medium-high. Needs you to sit down daily and re-plan without quitting.

### C. The Shutdown Ritual (close the day)
**What it's for:** stop lying awake with your brain chewing on unfinished work. Give it a real signal that the day is closed.

**Steps:**
1. At the end of work, look at what got done versus your plan.
2. Take every loose thread still floating in your head and write it somewhere trusted (calendar, list). Nothing stays in your head.
3. Write down the 1 to 3 things that matter tomorrow.
4. Say or tap a fixed phrase: "Shutdown complete." That phrase is the point. It tells your brain it's allowed to stop.

**His rules:** You must actually resolve the loose ends first, then close. The close has to be said or tapped, not just thought. Give it a month before it fully works.

**Energy:** Low-medium once it's a habit.

### D. The Six-List Cleanup (his "day one, chaos to calm")
**What it's for:** when your real workload is scattered across email, texts, your head, and it feels bigger and scarier than you can picture. This drags all of it into the open.

**Steps:**
1. Block a whole day, not 30 minutes. This is a real job.
2. Make six lists: **Ready** (can do now), **Waiting** (waiting on someone), **Backburner** (maybe later), **To Discuss**, **Clarify** (don't even know what it is yet), **Scheduled**.
3. Go through your whole inbox. Turn every email into a task on one of the six lists. Don't answer them, just sort them. Empty the inbox.
4. Do the same with your calendar and with whatever's floating in your head.
5. If you don't understand an item, drop it on **Clarify** and keep moving. Don't stop to think.
6. Then go through Clarify: throw some out, send a quick question on others (those move to Waiting), turn the rest into real tasks.
7. For the next four weeks: 5 minutes each morning reviewing the lists, a few minutes each evening closing loops, and once a week redo the full sort.

**His rules:** One task = one item that moves between lists. It never sits on two lists. Send "sorry, I can't do this" messages weeks early, not at the deadline. Do the daily/weekly upkeep for four weeks or it collapses.

**Energy:** High. A full day plus four weeks of upkeep.

### E. The Weekly Template
**What it's for:** stop re-inventing your whole week from scratch every Monday. Decide the shape of a good week once per season, then just apply it.

**Steps:**
1. At the start of a season (about 3 months), write your big goals for it.
2. Under that, write a bullet list of "what my ideal week looks like this season" (not a calendar, just categories).
3. Block recurring protected time for your top priority ("mornings till 10:30 are for writing, every weekday").
4. For each protected block, decide in advance what you'll do when it gets broken ("if a meeting eats my morning, I write at the library after").
5. Give days themes ("Monday = no meetings").
6. Set simple numeric limits ("two committees max, one podcast a week").
7. Each week, look at this template first and apply it, instead of starting blank.

**His rules:** Work at the season scale, not day-to-day, not five years out. Protect the recurring block even when it makes you a bit of a pain. Every protected block needs a backup plan for the thing that'll break it.

**Energy:** Medium, but it assumes you already plan weekly.

### F. The Deep Life Stack (rebuild your life in order)
**What it's for:** people who want a big change (quit the job, move) but keep failing because they build it on sand. This makes you build the foundation first.

**The four layers, strictly in this order:**
1. **Discipline.** Pick one or two small daily things you'll actually do (walk 20 minutes, read a bit). The goal isn't the task, it's proving to yourself "I'm a person who follows through." Also set up ONE place (a folder, a drawer) where everything you commit to gets written.
2. **Values.** Write what you stand for. Add small rituals that reconnect you to it (a weekly reflection, a walk). Add regular actions that live it out.
3. **Calm (control).** Build a real planner so you can see your actual workload. Then cut things until you have breathing room. Control first, then trim.
4. **Plan.** Only now, split life into areas (health, work, family), take one at a time, and ask what you want it to become. This is where the big moves go.

Then once a year (he ties it to your birthday) walk back through all four and fix whichever one needs it.

**His rules:** Strictly bottom-up. You cannot jump to the big Plan layer while the bottom is empty. Building the first three can take six months to a year. Discipline is an identity, not a chore. If depression is in the picture, get professional help first; this is not a replacement for therapy.

**Energy:** Medium, but it's a marathon. Months before the exciting payoff.

### G. Reverse Goal-Setting / Lifestyle Vision (for the big change)
**What it's for:** figuring out what you actually want your life to feel like, instead of chasing one dramatic move that "fixes everything" and then fizzles.

**Steps (the notebook version):**
1. Carry a small notebook. Whenever something makes you think "I'd want a life like that" (a scene, a person, a place), write it down.
2. Once a month, reread it and write a short list of what keeps mattering to you (quiet, mastery, time outdoors).
3. From that, write a first-person paragraph describing your ideal ordinary day about ten years out. The feel of it, not a job title.
4. Use that paragraph to judge real decisions: does this move me toward it?

**For a specific big goal, work backwards:** name the finished outcome, chain back to what it really needs, but only commit in detail to the first 30 days. And before you commit, talk to 2 or 3 real people who've done it. Believe what they actually did, not the story in your head.

**His rules:** Describe the life you want as feelings and rhythms you can check ("my mornings are unhurried"), never as one specific job or city. Only chase one or two big goals at once. A good goal is a "slayable dragon": a real stretch, but doable, not so huge it just shames you.

**Energy:** Medium, but slow. This pays off over seasons and years.

### H. Slow Productivity (do less, better)
**What it's for:** burnout from having far too much on your plate at once. Not "stop working," but "work on fewer things, at a human pace, done well."

**The three parts:**
1. **Do fewer things.** Cut your commitments below the overload line. New work waits in a queue and you pull it in only when you have room, instead of it landing on you.
2. **Work at a natural pace.** Hard stretches followed by real lighter stretches. Judge yourself over months and years, not days.
3. **Obsess over quality.** Pour the freed-up energy into doing the few things you kept really well.

**His rules:** The problem isn't activity, it's too much activity. Never run at 10-out-of-10 all day every day. Doing something excellently is what earns you the right to say no to everything else.

**Energy:** Medium, but it assumes you already have the power to say no. That's the catch.

## 2. The journey as one ladder everyone climbs differently

All of that becomes **one ladder of stones**, in Newport's own order. His rule is sacred: **prove you follow through on something tiny first, THEN add structure, THEN connect it to a big vision.** You never hand someone the top of the ladder.

Here are the rungs, bottom to top:

**Rung 1 — One tiny proof.** One small thing you actually do today. The point isn't the thing, it's proving "I'm someone who follows through." (From the Discipline layer and Time-Block "one real hour.")

**Rung 2 — Close the day.** Learn the shutdown: write the loose ends somewhere trusted, name tomorrow's top thing, say the closing phrase. (Shutdown ritual.)

**Rung 3 — Give the day a shape.** Plan the day in blocks. Give hours jobs instead of drifting. (Time-block planning.)

**Rung 4 — Give the week a shape.** The weekly look: what matters this week, roughly which day. (The load-bearing review.)

**Rung 5 — Do fewer things.** Learn to see your real load, say the firm no, drop the 20% nobody notices. (Slow productivity, saying no.)

**Rung 6 — Shape the season.** A 3-month direction that the weeks and days draw from. Declare growth / steady / recovery seasons. (Weekly template, seasons.)

**Rung 7 — Name what you're building toward.** Values, then a vision of the life you actually want. (Lifestyle vision.)

**Rung 8 — Make the big move, wisely.** Reverse-plan a real change, checked against real people who did it. (Reverse goal-setting.)

**The key point: everyone gets on at a different rung, and everyone can stop where it fits.** A frozen person may spend a year happy on Rung 1 and that's a full success. A sprawling high-capacity person may start around Rung 3 and climb to 8. The app's job is to find the right rung for each person and never shove them up before they're ready. Climbing is optional. The ladder is not a treadmill.

## 3. The four users, each one's path

This is the heart of it. For each person: their situation, what helps them, what would crush them, where they start, what the app gives them and when, and how it talks to them.

### David — the sprawling one
**Situation in one line:** high capacity, too many parallel goals (art, app, YouTube, design business, TV show, gym), plus avoidance of the messy room, the gym, and pot.

**What helps him:** almost the whole Newport toolkit fits, because his problem is Newport's own problem: real ability, scattered. **Slow Productivity** (do fewer things), the **Weekly Template**, **Time-Block Planning**, **seasons**, and **reverse goal-setting** for the big art/career moves.

**What would crush him:** nothing crushes him, but the wrong move is dumping ALL his goals into an active plan at once. That recreates the overwhelm. The failure mode for David is breadth, so the app must actively hold most goals in a "queue" and keep only one or two "active."

**Where he starts:** around **Rung 3-4** (day and week shape). He doesn't need the tiny-proof rung for its own sake, but he does need the WIP-cap discipline early.

**What the app gives him, in order:**
- First: the ability to park all his sprawling goals somewhere trusted so they're out of his head, and pick only 1 to 2 active ones. That itself is relief.
- Second: a weekly template and daily blocks so his irregular life (beach, grandma, Shabbat, travel) has shape without rigidity. Seasons handle the off-weeks.
- Later: reverse-planning for the genuinely big goals (portfolio, prints, TV show), each checked against real people.

**What it withholds:** it does not let him activate ten goals. It refuses. That refusal is the feature. It also doesn't moralize about the room or the pot; those become optional tiny keystones only if he opts in.

**Translated for him:** the full system, spoken to a capable adult who's just overloaded. Honest about horizons ("this pays off over quarters, not days").

### Mom — 66, Russian, fear-gated
**Situation in one line:** wants meaning and small growth (a book, less scrolling, gentle sports, lose weight), but the sports goal is blocked by a past trainer who was cruel to her.

**What helps her:** the **gentle keystone habit** ("one small daily thing"), the **resonance notebook** (write what moved you today), and the **lifestyle vision** as meaning, not hustle. A tiny, trainer-free on-ramp to movement (a walk, not a workout).

**What would crush her:** almost all the heavy machinery. The Six-List Cleanup, time-blocking, the Deep Life Stack, WIP-caps, discipline-metrics. She is not a knowledge worker drowning in email. Handing her Newport's productivity stack would be cruel and irrelevant. **Say it plainly: most of Newport does not fit Mom, because her challenge is emotional fear, not scattered workload.**

**Where she starts:** **Rung 1 only**, and maybe stays there happily. One tiny thing. For sports, the tiny thing is smaller than she thinks: put on shoes, walk to the corner. No trainer, no gym, no word "workout."

**What the app gives her, in order:**
- First: one gentle daily thing that feels like caring for herself, in Russian.
- Second: a way to note what resonates, feeding a soft sense of what she values.
- Later, only if she wants: a slightly longer walk. Growth by inches.

**What it withholds:** planning systems, metrics-tracking as pressure, any framing of life as output. And crucially, it withholds the scary version of the sports goal until the tiny version has proven safe.

**Translated for her:** gentle, Russian, meaning over hustle. The past wound is handled by shrinking the first step below the fear line and never mentioning trainers or performance. Weight is never nagged; movement is framed as kindness to herself.

### Sister — two kids, no energy, house is chaos
**Situation in one line:** wants the house clean and to lose weight, but cleaning is so huge it freezes her, and she has no energy.

**What helps her:** exactly ONE mechanic, and it's the deepest Newport idea applied kindly: **your brain won't start a plan it doesn't trust, so shrink the plan until it does.** The "one real thing" shrunk to almost nothing. The app finds the single smallest first step ("just the kitchen sink," then "just the floor") and walks her through one micro-step at a time.

**What would crush her:** everything with structure. Six lists, time-blocks, weekly templates, seasons, vision paragraphs. She has no energy and no bandwidth; any system is one more thing to fail at. **Say it plainly: Newport's productivity systems are the wrong tool for Sister. They assume spare capacity she doesn't have.** Only his softest, smallest idea survives.

**Where she starts:** **Rung 1, and the app never even shows her the ladder.** She sees one tiny step, then the next.

**What the app gives her, in order:**
- First: the single smallest possible step, chosen for her, presented alone. Not a plan. One thing.
- Second: warm acknowledgment when it's done, then the next single step. Never a list of steps.
- Later: nothing added unless energy visibly returns. The win is momentum, not a system.

**What it withholds:** the whole plan. She must never see the mountain, only the next pebble. And the word "productivity" never appears.

**Translated for her:** never the language of productivity or systems. The tone is a friend standing next to her in the messy kitchen saying "just this one drawer, I've got you." One tiny step only, ever.

### Brother — ADHD, avoidant, scared of the footage
**Situation in one line:** big dreams (famous musician and filmmaker), but ADHD, low self-esteem, and fear that makes him avoid, like being scared to even open old film footage he needs to edit.

**What helps him:** the same "shrink it below the fear line" move as Sister, plus a **focus-help mechanic**: one thing at a time, a short timer, a way to pull a distractible mind back. Tiny wins that rebuild "I can do this." The interval training idea (start at 10 minutes of one thing) fits him, gently.

**What would crush him:** long multi-month sequencing, the full Deep Life Stack, anything with lots of parts to maintain. His executive function is the bottleneck. **Say it plainly: the big Newport stacks would become another abandoned system for him.** He needs the smallest on-ramp, not a program.

**Where he starts:** **Rung 1**, framed as shrinking the scary task, with focus support layered in.

**What the app gives him, in order:**
- First: take the terrifying task ("edit the footage") and shrink it until it's below the fear line. "Just open the folder." "Just watch 30 seconds." The threatening thing gets small enough to touch.
- Second: a focus companion for the actual doing. One thing, short timer, gentle nudge back when he drifts. A tiny win logged as real evidence he did it.
- Later: slightly bigger reps as confidence grows. Interval by interval.

**What it withholds:** the whole project, the big plan, the long horizon. He sees only the next tiny below-threshold action. No streak-shame if he drops off; a season can pause.

**Translated for him:** shrink the scary thing, help him focus, celebrate the tiny win as proof he's not who his old fear says he is. Never a lecture, never a long list.

## 4. How the app inspires each of them (the voice)

Newport's kindest ideas, aimed at each person. The three that carry the most warmth:
- **You're not lazy. Your attention was stolen.**
- **Your brain won't start a plan it doesn't trust, so we'll make the plan tiny enough to trust.**
- **Do less, better.**

### To David
Aim it at the overload, not his character. Give him permission to park things.
- "You've got ten fires. We're going to light one on purpose and let the other nine wait where you can see them. Nothing's lost."
- "This one won't pay off this week. It pays off this season. That's not slow, that's how the real stuff gets built."

### To Mom (gentle, meaning, Russian in the app)
Warm, no pressure, about caring for herself.
- "Today, just the walk to the corner and back. That's the whole thing. No one is watching, no one is grading you."
- "You showed up for yourself today. That's the part that matters, and you did it."

### To Sister (never the word productivity)
A friend in the kitchen. One pebble.
- "Forget the whole house. Right now it's just the sink. Just the sink. I'm right here."
- "That's done. You're not stuck, look, you just moved. One more tiny thing when you're ready, or rest. Both are fine."

### To Brother (shrink the fear, rebuild belief)
Gentle, tiny, and quietly reminding him he's capable.
- "You don't have to edit it. Just open the folder and look at one clip. That's the entire job right now."
- "You just did the thing you were scared of, even a little. That fear that says you can't? It was wrong this time. Log it. That's proof."

## What to build first (the smallest real first step)

Build the **"shrink the scary/big thing to one trusted tiny step, and present that step ALONE"** engine, wired into the existing journey and mood check.

Concretely, the smallest version that makes this real:
1. When a person names a goal, the app asks (or infers) the ONE smallest first action and shows only that, not the plan behind it.
2. The mood/energy check routes it: low energy or fear-flagged goal means the step gets shrunk further and the word "productivity" and any system-talk is suppressed. Higher capacity (David) unlocks the park-your-goals-and-pick-one-or-two view instead.
3. On completion, a warm close that names it as evidence ("you did the thing you avoided"), never a grade.

That single mechanic serves three of the four users directly (Mom, Sister, Brother) and is the honest floor David's overload also needs. It's the "smallest plan your brain will trust" idea turned into a feature. Everything else on the ladder can be layered on top later, rung by rung.
